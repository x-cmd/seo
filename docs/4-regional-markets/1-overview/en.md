# Regional Search Engines — When Google Is Not the Default

**The short answer:** Google handles about 90% of worldwide
searches, so "just do Google" is right more often than not. But in
roughly one market in ten, a local engine takes a real share — in
Russia nearly three times Google's, and in Korea more searches than
Google on the query-volume count — and the reasons are worth
understanding on their own terms. This page explains which
markets those are, how a local engine actually works, and how to read
the share numbers that describe them.

> **TL;DR.** Google ≈ 90% worldwide. The exceptions are Russia (Yandex
> ≈ 73%), Korea (Naver ≈ 46–63% depending on how you count), Taiwan
> (Yahoo! ≈ 13%), the Czech Republic (Seznam ≈ 12%), Japan
> (Yahoo! ≈ 9%) and Vietnam (Cốc Cốc ≈ 4%). Three things make a local
> engine worth caring about: it has its **own index**, it has **its own
> webmaster tool**, and its users **search differently**. If a market
> fails any of those three tests, optimizing for Google is enough.

## Do I actually need to optimize for another engine?

Ask yourself this first: *where are my users, and do they search the
way their market searches?*

Most sites sell to one country. If that country is the United States,
the United Kingdom, Germany, France, India, Brazil, or most of
Southeast Asia, then Google plus one or two of its satellites is the
whole story. If your users are in Russia, Korea, the Czech Republic,
Vietnam, Japan or Taiwan, the answer is different, and this page
explains exactly how.

The trap is that "local" does not automatically mean "worth optimizing
for." A local engine only earns your effort when it has an **index you
cannot borrow**. If a local engine simply re-serves Google's results
with a different skin, then ranking on Google already covers it, and
building a second strategy buys you nothing but work.

Read it as a list of leftovers. Google takes nine out of every ten
queries; everything else together is about one in ten. Bing is second
at 4% and the reason is not user love — it is that Bing ships as the
default search engine on Windows and Edge, and many people never
change it. Yandex is third at 2.2% almost entirely because it owns the
Russian market. Everything below 1% is rounding error globally, even
though several of those engines dominate their home market.

That is the whole lesson of this chapter in one chart: **a global share
number is not a market share number.** An engine at 0.3% worldwide can
be the majority engine in one country.

## Three shapes a local engine can take

Once you find a market where a local engine matters, the next question
is what kind of engine it is. There are only three shapes, and they
have very different consequences for your work.

### 1. The independent index

The engine crawls the web itself and ranks what it finds. Naver,
Yandex, Baidu, Seznam and Cốc Cốc all work this way.

This is the only shape that requires a second SEO strategy. If the
engine has its own crawler, its own ranking signals, and its own
webmaster tool, then submitting to Google and hoping is not enough —
you have to be indexed there too, on its terms.

### 2. The licensed wrapper

The engine borrows somebody else's index and adds its own interface,
its own verticals, and sometimes its own ranking layer. Yahoo! JAPAN
serves results from Google's index; Yahoo! Taiwan does the same;
Ecosia runs on Bing's index while adding its own climate data.

This shape is much cheaper to cover. Your Google work largely carries
over. What does not carry over is everything about *presentation*:
which result appears first, whether you get a rich result card, how
your title and description are truncated, and whether a whole vertical
of the engine's own ecosystem sits in front of the organic list.

### 3. The meta-search

The engine sends your query to other engines and merges what comes
back. Startpage, MetaGer and Qwant have all operated this way at some
point.

For an optimizer this is nearly free. If Startpage proxies Google,
your Google ranking is your Startpage ranking. There is nothing extra
to submit and no separate tool to learn.

in a table, too small to justify a separate strategy. They matter as
a signal about the privacy conversation in those countries, and
because two of them run on Bing's index, which means they ride along
with the Bing work you would do anyway.

## Why the numbers disagree — read the measurement, not just the figure

If you look up Korea's market share you will find at least three
different answers. All of them are real. They measure different things.

**StatCounter** counts the *referral* — the clicks that leave the
search engine for a website. **InternetTrend** counts *queries*,
including the ones answered entirely on the engine's own pages.

Korea is where the difference is largest, because Naver answers a huge
share of Korean questions inside Naver: its Knowledge iN answers, its
blog results, its cafe threads, its place listings. A Korean user can
get a complete answer without ever clicking through to your site — so
StatCounter never sees the visit.

For 2025, the two sources say:

| Engine | InternetTrend (queries) | StatCounter (referrals) |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Naver | 62.9% | 45.6% |
| Google | 29.6% | 45.8% |
| Bing | 3.1% | 5.0% |
| Daum | 2.9% | 1.2% |

A 17-point swing on the same market, same year. Which one is "right"
depends on what you are trying to measure. If you are asking *how
many searches could I win*, use the query number. If you are asking
*how many visitors could I get*, use the referral number — or better,
use your own analytics.

The same trap applies everywhere. When a blog states a share, look
for the method before you act on it. Numbers that arrive without a
source, a date, and a measurement basis are usually wrong — and
numbers that contradict two independent trackers are usually wrong
too.

## What actually changes when you enter a local market

Passing the importance test is only the start. A market with a local
champion usually differs from the US in four concrete ways.

**Language is not translation.** A Korean or Czech searcher types the
way a local person asks a local question, not a translated keyword.
Korean queries are frequently full natural sentences; Czech queries
carry diacritics that get stripped by careless URL design. Machine
translation reads as machine translation, both to the user and to the
engine.

**The result page is not a list of links.** Naver assembles a *Smart
Block* from its own verticals — blog, cafe, place, shopping,
knowledge answers. The top of the page is not your website. On
Naver, being present *inside* the vertical ecosystem matters as much
as ranking on the organic page.

**Submission is a separate job.** Google Search Console does nothing
for Naver, Seznam or Yandex. Each has its own verification flow, its
own sitemaps endpoint, and sometimes its own indexing protocol. Seznam
and Yandex both accept [IndexNow](https://www.indexnow.org/), which
makes getting indexed fast a solved problem rather than a wait.

**Links mean something different.** In a market where the local engine
ranks its own index, a link from a local directory or a local news
site carries far more weight than the same link from an international
site would in Google. Czech-language links from Czech sites are the
currency.

## How to tell whether a local engine matters

Six questions, asked in order, separate the markets where a second
engine changes something from the markets where it changes nothing:

1. **How big is the market in your traffic?** Below roughly 5% of
   sessions, a country's local engine cannot move its numbers much,
   whatever its home share.
2. **Does the local engine have its own index?** If it re-serves
   Google or Bing results, the Google and Bing work already reaches
   it — there is nothing separate to do.
3. **Does it have its own webmaster tool?** Naver, Seznam, Yandex,
   Baidu and Bing each run one; the engines without a tool are, without
   exception, riding somebody else's index.
4. **Does it have its own vertical ecosystem?** Naver, Daum, Kakao,
   Cốc Cốc and Seznam all have products where visibility means being
   present inside them, not just ranking in web results.
5. **Is the query language different?** Korean, Japanese, Czech and
   Vietnamese queries are sentences in the local language; a
   machine-translated page answers them badly.
6. **Do several engines share one submission?** Bing's index serves
   Yahoo, DuckDuckGo and Ecosia; IndexNow reaches Bing, Yandex and
   Seznam together.
